Stuffed and Bloated After Thanksgiving Dinner? What Chinese Medicine Says (and 5 Things That Actually Help)

Chinese medicine has had a name for the post-feast slump for two thousand years. Here is what it is, four acupressure points for it, and what the research says about the walk, the ginger tea and the after-dinner drink.

By Dr. Stacy Peck, DACM, L.Ac.

Thanksgiving is Thursday, November 26. By about 4 p.m. a good share of the country will be on the couch with a tight waistband, a belly that feels like a drum, and no interest in pie that they will eat anyway.

Chinese medicine has a specific name for that feeling and a short list of things to do about it. Some of that list now has modern research behind it, and one popular remedy turns out not to work at all. This post covers both, with every claim checked against the classical text, a standard Chinese medicine textbook, or a published study listed at the end.

The short answer

If you are reading this from the couch: stand up and take a slow 10 to 15 minute walk, sip something warm (fresh ginger tea is the classic), skip the after-dinner drink, and press the four points below for a minute or two each. Then give your stomach a light day tomorrow.

What Chinese medicine calls it: food stagnation

In Chinese medicine the Stomach receives food and “rots and ripens” it, then sends it downward. The Spleen, its partner, transforms what you ate into usable energy and moves it around the body.1 Both jobs have a capacity. Overload them in one sitting and food sits in the middle instead of moving on. The textbook name is retention of food, often called food stagnation.

Maciocia’s description of the pattern reads like a Thanksgiving evening: fullness and distension in the upper belly, belching, sour regurgitation, nausea, no desire to eat, and a thick coating on the tongue. Overeating is the classic cause.1 When the Stomach cannot send things down, its qi goes the wrong way, which is the belching and the queasiness.

This is an old observation. The Suwen, the foundational text of Chinese medicine, says in its forty-third chapter that when food and drink are doubled, the Stomach and Intestines are injured.2 Two thousand years later, the advice that follows from it is still the same: get things moving downward again, and stop adding to the pile.

Five things that help, in order

  1. Walk for 10 to 15 minutes. Slowly. This is the one with the best modern evidence. In a small German study, ten healthy men ate a standard meal and then either rested or walked slowly; the walk shortened the time for half the meal to leave the stomach from about 123 minutes to about 107.3 In a randomized trial of people with ongoing bloating, a 10 to 15 minute walk after each meal for four weeks improved belching, gas and after-meal fullness, and did better than the comparison medication (domperidone with an anti-gas agent) on fullness and bloating.4 A caveat: in the German study the walkers’ stomachs emptied faster but they did not report feeling less full that evening. A walk helps things move; it is not instant relief.
  2. Sip something warm. Chinese dietary therapy treats the Stomach as a pot on a stove: it needs warmth to do its work, and iced drinks on top of a heavy meal are thought to slow it down.5 Warm water is enough. Fresh ginger tea is better (next item).
  3. Make fresh ginger tea. Fresh ginger (Sheng Jiang) is the classic Chinese kitchen remedy to warm the Stomach and settle nausea.5 In a small randomized study, 1.2 grams of ginger made the stomach empty faster than placebo (half-emptying in about 12 minutes versus 16 for a test soup). The same study found no difference in how people felt, so treat it as a gentle nudge, not a cure.6 To make it: four or five thin slices of fresh ginger, a cup of just-boiled water, ten minutes.
  4. Use the traditional “rich meal” foods. Chinese dietary therapy matches the food that got stuck with the food that moves it. Hawthorn berry (Shan Zha) is the traditional choice after meat and greasy food, radish (daikon) after too much starch and bread, and a strip of dried tangerine peel (Chen Pi) in hot water for the bloated, gassy feeling.5 These are traditional uses; they have not been tested in holiday-meal trials. Hawthorn can interact with heart and blood pressure medication, so skip it if you take those.
  5. Press the four points below. A minute or two each, sitting upright.

Four acupressure points for a too-full stomach

These four are not a random selection. When researchers pooled the acupuncture trials for chronic indigestion (functional dyspepsia), the three points used most often were Stomach 36, Pericardium 6 and Ren 12, usually together.7 Locations and traditional indications below are from Deadman’s A Manual of Acupuncture.8

  1. Pericardium 6 (Nei Guan) — inner forearm, three finger-widths above the wrist crease, between the two tendons. The point for nausea and a Stomach that is sending things up instead of down. Press firmly with your thumb, 1 to 2 minutes each wrist. Start here if you feel queasy.
  2. Stomach 36 (Zu San Li) — four finger-widths below the bottom edge of the kneecap, one finger-width to the outside of the shin bone. The main point on the Stomach channel, used for fullness, distension and poor digestion of every kind. Press or rub in small circles, 1 to 2 minutes each leg.
  3. Ren 12 (Zhong Wan) — on the midline of the belly, halfway between the navel and the bottom of the breastbone. The Stomach’s own alarm point. After a big meal, do not press hard here. Lay a warm palm over it and make slow, light clockwise circles for a minute or two.
  4. Stomach 25 (Tian Shu) — three finger-widths to either side of the navel. The Large Intestine’s alarm point, used for lower-belly bloating and sluggish bowels. Same rule: light clockwise circles with flat fingers, both sides at once.

What the evidence does and does not say. A 2021 review of 22 randomized trials found that adding acupuncture to standard medication improved chronic indigestion more than medication alone, and the authors rated most of those trials as low quality.9 Those trials used needles, in people with months of symptoms. Nobody has run a trial of self-acupressure after a holiday dinner. These points are safe, free and traditional; that is the honest claim.

If you are pregnant, skip the two belly points and check with your provider before using acupressure.

What does not help

The digestif. The same German study that tested the walk also tested the after-dinner drink. Brandy, herbal liqueur, aquavit and espresso were each given after the meal. None of them changed how fast the stomach emptied, and the alcoholic drinks made no difference to how full people felt.3 If you enjoy it, enjoy it. It is not helping you digest.

Lying flat on the couch. In Chinese medicine the Stomach’s whole job is to send food down.1 Lying flat with a full stomach does not help it. Sit upright, or better, go for the walk.

Going back for thirds to “settle your stomach.” See the Suwen, above.

The day after

Chinese dietary therapy’s answer to an overworked Stomach is a day of simple, warm, cooked food: rice porridge (congee), soup, steamed vegetables, and smaller portions.5 Not a fast, not a cleanse, and not a cold green smoothie. Keep the leftovers for Saturday.

Common questions

What is the best pressure point for bloating after eating?

Stomach 36 (Zu San Li), four finger-widths below the kneecap and one finger-width outside the shin bone, is the most widely used point for fullness and bloating. Add Pericardium 6 on the inner wrist if you also feel nauseous.

Does a drink after dinner help digestion?

No. In a controlled study, brandy, herbal liqueur, aquavit and espresso after a meal did not speed stomach emptying, and the alcoholic drinks did not reduce fullness. A slow walk did speed emptying.3

Does walking after a big meal help bloating?

Yes, modestly. A slow walk after eating speeds stomach emptying, and in one randomized trial a 10 to 15 minute walk after meals for four weeks reduced bloating and after-meal fullness.3,4

What does Chinese medicine call overeating?

Retention of food, or food stagnation: food sitting in the Stomach instead of moving downward, with fullness, belching, sour regurgitation and nausea.1

When to call a doctor

Feeling overfull after a very large meal is normal and passes. Get medical help right away for severe or worsening belly pain, chest pain or pressure, repeated vomiting, vomiting blood, black or bloody stools, or a hard, swollen belly. See your provider if bloating shows up most days, comes with weight loss you did not intend, or does not go away.
